# Chicago Manual of Style: adopted editorial principles

## Purpose

This file records the Chicago-derived editorial principles adopted by the editorial-style skill.

It is **not a reproduction or substitute for The Chicago Manual of Style**. Chicago remains a reference authority for questions of general editorial mechanics when the decision register and Microsoft guidance do not provide a specific rule.

As of September 22, 2026, the current edition is **The Chicago Manual of Style, 18th edition**.

## Authority and precedence

Apply these authorities in this order:

1. The house rules and decision register (`../decision-register.md`)
2. Microsoft Writing Style Guide for Microsoft terminology and Microsoft-specific technical conventions
3. Chicago for general editorial mechanics and publishing questions
4. Merriam-Webster for spelling and word forms when higher-priority authorities are silent

Chicago should not override:

- official Microsoft product names
- official UI labels
- code or command syntax
- Azure resource names
- proper names of people and organizations
- established project terminology

## Role in this skill

Use Chicago primarily to resolve questions involving:

- grammar and usage
- punctuation
- spelling and compounds
- names and titles
- numbers
- abbreviations
- quotations
- source citations
- tables and illustrations
- manuscript and publication consistency

The objective is not to make documents look academic. The objective is to provide a stable editorial fallback for matters that technical style guides may not address.

## General mechanics

### Prefer syntactic clarity

Use punctuation to clarify relationships between ideas, not to preserve an unnecessarily complicated sentence.

If a sentence requires several commas, semicolons, parentheses, or dashes to remain understandable, first determine whether the sentence should be decomposed.

Punctuation is not a substitute for information architecture.

### Use punctuation consistently

Apply one convention consistently within a document unless content type requires an exception.

Pay particular attention to:

- commas in series
- punctuation around parenthetical material
- colon use before explanations or lists
- semicolons between closely related independent clauses
- em dashes for interruption or emphasis
- en dashes for appropriate ranges or relationships
- quotation-mark treatment
- end punctuation in lists

When the decision register or Microsoft has an explicit rule, follow that rule.

## Ranges and spans

Use a consistent, readable treatment for ranges.

For compact numeric or date ranges, an en dash may be appropriate:

> August 23–September 21, 2026

In running prose, words may be clearer:

> from August 23 through September 21, 2026

Do not combine *from* with an en-dash construction.

Choose the form that best supports readability and consistency in the document.

## Parentheses and dashes

Use parenthetical material sparingly.

If information is important enough to understand the main point, consider integrating it into the sentence or giving it its own sentence.

Use dashes deliberately. Avoid relying on repeated dashes to hold together several distinct thoughts.

For technical prose, structural decomposition usually takes precedence over dramatic punctuation.

## Numbers, dates, and units

Follow the decision register and Microsoft conventions first for:

- dates
- times
- percentages
- measurements
- version numbers
- technical quantities
- Azure pricing
- capacity and throughput values

Use Chicago as the fallback for general-number treatment when no technical convention applies.

Within a document:

- present comparable quantities consistently
- do not alternate arbitrarily between words and numerals
- make units explicit when ambiguity is possible
- use unambiguous date forms
- preserve precision that matters to the technical conclusion

## Abbreviations and acronyms

Prefer established project and industry abbreviations only when they help the intended audience.

Unless an abbreviation is broadly understood by the intended audience or explicitly exempted by house style:

1. write the term in full at first meaningful use
2. provide the abbreviation in parentheses
3. use the abbreviation consistently afterward

Do not create abbreviations merely to shorten a term that appears only a few times.

Microsoft-specific abbreviation guidance takes precedence for Microsoft technologies.

## Names, terms, and titles

Preserve official names and capitalization.

Do not normalize a proper name to fit a generic editorial preference.

Examples include:

- Microsoft product and service names
- organization names
- project names
- policy names
- formal document titles
- Azure resource identifiers

When a formal title and a generic description both appear, distinguish them consistently.

## Quotations

Use quotations when the exact wording matters.

Prefer paraphrase when the goal is to summarize an idea rather than preserve the author's wording.

When quoting:

- preserve the meaning accurately
- identify the source when required
- avoid unnecessary long quotations
- distinguish quoted material clearly from surrounding analysis
- use one quotation convention consistently

In technical and planning documents, quotations should normally support analysis rather than replace it.

## Citations and source references

If a document requires formal citation, select a citation system and apply it consistently.

Chicago supports multiple citation systems. Choose one system per document rather than mixing conventions.

Possible approaches include:

- footnotes/endnotes plus bibliography
- author-date references
- a project-specific source-note format
- a concise "Sources reviewed" section when formal scholarly citation is unnecessary

By default, use the decision register's Sources block.

### Source-list consistency

Within a source list, keep entries parallel.

For example, use a predictable pattern such as:

> Source or artifact, relevant scope, date

or:

> Artifact title, system or location, date reviewed

Do not mix complete sentences, fragments, URLs, and informal notes without an intentional structure.

## Tables and figures

Use a table when readers need to compare attributes across multiple items.

Do not use a table merely to reduce page length.

A useful table should have:

- a clear purpose
- understandable column headings
- consistent units
- parallel entries
- enough context to stand on its own
- accessible reading order in the final output format

Use prose or lists when the information does not benefit from row-and-column comparison.

Figures should have meaningful titles or captions when the surrounding context does not fully identify their purpose.

## Capitalization

Follow the decision register and Microsoft capitalization rules first.

Avoid unnecessary capitalization used for emphasis.

Do not capitalize generic technical concepts merely because they are important.

Preserve capitalization in:

- official product names
- formal program names
- project-defined terms when intentionally treated as proper names
- code and identifiers exactly as required

## Spelling, compounds, and hyphenation

Use the following decision order:

1. Project terminology and the decision register
2. official Microsoft or vendor terminology
3. Merriam-Webster
4. Chicago guidance for unresolved editorial treatment

Do not alter established technical spellings to satisfy a generic compound-word preference.

## Consistency as an editorial requirement

When multiple acceptable forms exist, choose one and use it consistently.

This applies to:

- date presentation
- range presentation
- abbreviations
- list punctuation
- capitalization
- table headings
- source references
- quotation treatment
- figure captions
- terminology

Consistency should not override factual accuracy or official naming.

## Relationship to information decomposition

Chicago governs mechanics. It doesn't justify over-compressed prose.

If a sentence is difficult to punctuate because it contains too many independent ideas:

> Rewrite the sentence before refining the punctuation.

The house rule remains:

> Give each sentence a clear job and each paragraph one primary informational purpose.

## Editorial checks

Use Chicago as a review layer by asking:

1. Is punctuation clarifying the sentence rather than compensating for complexity?
2. Are ranges, dates, and numbers presented consistently?
3. Are abbreviations introduced and used consistently?
4. Are official names preserved exactly?
5. Are quotations necessary and correctly distinguished?
6. Does the document use one consistent source-reference method?
7. Are list, table, and figure conventions consistent?
8. Are capitalization and compound-word decisions intentional?
9. Does any editorial treatment conflict with Microsoft terminology?
10. Should an edge case be resolved by consulting the current Chicago Manual directly?
